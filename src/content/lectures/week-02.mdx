## Why this week exists

Last week's first move was **choose characters**. We skipped the hard part: you
cannot choose from nothing.

Linnaeus had it easy. Every flower has stamens, so "how many stamens" is
available for every case before you start. Material that was not written for you
rarely hands you anything that clean. Today is how you find candidates anyway,
and how you tell a usable one from a plausible one.

This afternoon you do it on the 山海经. The hour is the general version.

## By the end of the hour

You should be able to look at unfamiliar material and list three candidate
characters. You should be able to disqualify a candidate and say which of the
three tests it failed. And you should be able to say what your system does when
the material is silent.

## Outline

## Candidates come from repetition

You do not invent characters. You notice that the material keeps saying the same
kind of thing, and you make a column out of it.

So the first pass over any material is not analysis. It is counting. Which
phrases recur? Which appear in most records, which in a few? A shape that
repeats is a candidate. A shape that appears twice is an anecdote.

This is why the session this afternoon starts by counting rather than by
reading closely. Close reading of one record tells you about that record.

## Three tests

A candidate has to survive all three.

**Comparable.** You can check it on every case and get an answer of the same
kind. "Number of legs" is comparable. "Impressive" is not.

**Checkable.** Someone else, looking at the same material, gets the same answer.
If two readers disagree and neither is wrong, it is not a character yet.

**About the thing.** It describes what the thing is, not what it does for you,
not who owns it, not where you found it. This is the test Borges' encyclopedist
fails fourteen times in a row.

<Caution label="The most useful-looking candidate is usually relational">
  The characters that feel most informative are often about the relationship
  between the thing and a person. What it is worth, what it is for, what happens
  to you.

  They feel informative because they are the ones you would act on. That is
  exactly why they are not characters of the thing. Weeks 5 and 6 take two of
  them apart at length.
</Caution>

## Missing is not zero

Real material is incomplete. Your system will meet records that simply do not
say.

There are three different things a blank cell can mean, and a system that stores
them identically has destroyed information:

- the material says the value is nothing
- the material does not say
- nobody has checked yet

Only the first is a value. The other two are states of your own knowledge, and
they belong in a different column from the data.

<Caution label="Writing none is a claim">
  If the record is silent and your table says `none`, you have added a fact the
  material does not support. You will not remember, six weeks later, that you
  wrote it. Neither will a reader.

  This is the single thing the first assessment fails outright, and it is not a
  technicality. A taxonomy that cannot tell its own inferences from its sources
  is not evidence of anything.
</Caution>

## Name the column before you trust it

You will name your columns today and keep those names all semester. That feels
premature. It is deliberate.

A column with a name is a commitment you can argue with. A column with no name
gets quietly widened every time something does not fit, until it holds four
unrelated kinds of thing and nobody noticed when that happened.

Name it, write down what you think belongs in it, and let the next ten weeks
attack that definition.
