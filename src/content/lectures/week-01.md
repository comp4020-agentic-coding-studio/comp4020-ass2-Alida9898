The argument of the course, stated once at the start so the rest of the semester
can be checked against it.

A classification is a claim about what things are kinds of. Building one for the
山海经 (Shānhǎijīng) is not a stunt, because the text already classifies. The
work is in the disagreement between its principle and ours.

## Outline

- What Linnaeus assumed, and which of those assumptions the 山海经 denies
- The text's own order, which is geographic, and the question it answers well
- Borges on Wilkins, and why an arbitrary taxonomy is still a falsifiable one
- The three phases: build the system, stress it, break it
- The one rule that does not bend: cite the line, or say which line you cannot
  find

## The claim

Two classifications of the same material can both be correct and still be
incompatible, because they answer different questions. The geographic filing
answers what a traveller will meet. A taxonomy answers what a thing is a kind
of. Nothing in the text is trying to answer the second question, so every
character we extract is one we imposed. The course's method is to impose them
anyway and keep a written record of each imposition.

## What the three phases mean

Weeks 1 to 4 build a system that works. Weeks 5 to 8 apply it where it is
uncomfortable: to omens, to uses, to the boundary between a god and a beast, and
to a name that outlived its record. Weeks 9 to 12 apply it where it does not
work at all, and then ask what to do about that.

A course arranged this way has to be honest about week 9. If the system never
breaks, the last four weeks are theatre. The assessment is built to make the
break visible rather than survivable.
