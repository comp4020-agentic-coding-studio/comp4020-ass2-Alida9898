You already classify things. You have a folder structure, a way of sorting
laundry, a mental shelf for people you have met once. Most of these you built
without deciding anything, and most of them fail in the same way when they fail:
two things that belong together end up apart, and you cannot say why.

This course is about doing it on purpose. Twelve weeks, one worked example — the
山海经 (Shānhǎijīng, the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*), a book that files
hundreds of creatures and never says what any of them is a kind of. By week 12
you will build a system for material nobody has handed you a system for.

Today is the toolkit. No 山海经 yet; that is the session, this afternoon.

## By the end of the hour

You should be able to say what a classification system is made of, name the
three parts, and say which part is missing from a given attempt. You should be
able to look at a category and say whether it is a character or something else
wearing a character's clothes.

## The three moves

Before Linnaeus, a plant's name was a diagnostic phrase — a string of Latin
adjectives long enough to tell it from its neighbours. Branching habit, leaf
margin, whether the stem was hairless, all in the name. It is a description
pretending to be a label. It grows whenever somebody finds a similar plant, it
differs between authors, and you cannot alphabetise it.

Linnaeus did three things.

**He chose characters.** A small set of features you can check on every
specimen: how many stamens, does it have mammary glands, how many toes. The
choice is the whole game, and it is a choice — he sorted plants by their sexual
parts, which was not obvious and not uncontroversial.

**He nested them into ranks.** Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus,
species. Each one inside the last, so a claim made high up holds for everything
below it.

**He gave two-part names.** Genus plus species. *Homo sapiens*. One name, one
kind, everywhere, in a language nobody's nation owned.

What does the nesting actually buy? Economy.

Say *Homo sapiens* and you have also said primate, mammal, chordate, animal,
without writing any of it down. Six statements ride along with the name. A flat
list of names carries none of that, which is why a folder structure with two
hundred folders at the top level is unusable and one with six is not.

## The list that gets it wrong

In 1942 Borges wrote an essay on John Wilkins, who had tried to build a
universal language where the word for a thing encoded its category. Partway
through, Borges quotes a Chinese encyclopedia that does not exist, the Celestial
Emporium of Benevolent Knowledge, which divides animals into fourteen classes.
Among them: those belonging to the Emperor, embalmed ones, suckling pigs,
mermaids, stray dogs, those drawn with a very fine camel-hair brush, those that
resemble flies from a distance — and those included in this classification.

Foucault put it on the first page of *Les mots et les choses* and said he
laughed out loud.

Why is it funny? Not because the animals are strange; mermaids and suckling pigs
are no odder than a platypus. It is funny because every category is sorting by a
different kind of question. Who owns it. What state it is in. What it looks like
from far away. Whether it is on this list. Linnaeus counted stamens every single
time. Borges' encyclopedist changes the question with every entry, and a
category that sorts by membership in its own list is not about animals at all.

That gives you a test you will use for the rest of the semester. Take any
category and ask what question it answers. If two categories in the same system
answer different kinds of question, the system has a level problem.

## Arbitrary is not the same as broken

The Borges list is usually wheeled out to prove that all classification is
arbitrary, therefore no system is better than any other.

Watch what that argument does to itself. The joke only works because you can
tell the Emporium is bad. You knew it in one reading. So you were applying a
standard, which means a standard exists, which means classifications can be
better and worse.

Arbitrary means the boundary could have been drawn elsewhere. Almost every
boundary is arbitrary in that sense, including the species. Broken means the
system cannot do the job it claims to do. Those are different accusations and
only the second one is fatal. Keep them apart, especially in weeks 9 to 12 when
we start dismantling our own system.

## How the course runs

Three phases. Weeks 1 to 4 you build a system that works, and it will work
better than you expect. Weeks 5 to 8 you push it into places where it is
uncomfortable. Weeks 9 to 12 you take it apart, look at what other traditions
did instead, and design your own.

Every week states exactly one rule and tests it against at least three
creatures. Three, because two can always be picked to agree.

One rule never bends: you cite the line, or you declare which line you could not
find. A plausible-sounding quotation with nothing behind it is the single thing
that fails an assessment outright. We are building a system for a text that
cannot be re-examined — there is no specimen in a drawer — so the citation is
all the evidence there is.
