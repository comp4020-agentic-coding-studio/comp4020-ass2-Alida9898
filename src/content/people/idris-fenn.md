Trained on transmission rather than on animals. His work is the history of a
text as an object: which recension a reading belongs to, which hand added it,
and what a commentator's note is doing when it looks like part of the text.

On this course he is the brake. The rule that no line is quoted unless it was
read in the edition is his, and he marks the weekly cards against it. He is also
the source of the findings that cost the course its tidiest results. The same
name is printed with different graphs in different chapters —
<span lang="zh-Hant">肥遺</span> and <span lang="zh-Hant">肥𧔥</span>
(féiyí), <span lang="zh-Hant">讙頭</span> and <span lang="zh-Hant">驩頭</span>
(huāntóu), <span lang="zh-Hant">狌狌</span> and
<span lang="zh-Hant">猩猩</span> (xīngxīng) — and whether those are spellings of one
creature or names of several is a question about scribes before it is a question
about bodies.
<span lang="zh-Hant">郭璞</span>'s interlinear notes are stripped from every
quoted line for the same reason: a fourth-century gloss is evidence about the
fourth century.

He teaches the week on the record's fixed formula, and he supervises the
citation half of the final congress. A card that quotes a line nobody can find
fails on his marking, whatever else it does well.

Ask him about editions, variants, commentaries, and what a citation has to
carry.
