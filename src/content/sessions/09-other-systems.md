Week 9 left the system broken. Before rebuilding it, this week looks at two
bestiaries that were never built the way ours was, and asks the uncomfortable
question in the other direction: not whether they are worse, but whether ours
has been one of them all along.

## Two systems that never needed a species concept

The medieval European bestiary descends from the *Physiologus*. Its entries
describe an animal and then say what the animal means. The lion, the pelican
and the phoenix sit in the same book as the elephant on equal terms, because
the criterion for inclusion is signification and not existence. Nobody in that
tradition is embarrassed by the phoenix. Signification is the field, and the
phoenix fills it well.

Japanese yōkai catalogues sort differently again. 鳥山石燕's 画図百鬼夜行 and
its successors give each creature a name, a picture, and often nothing else.
The unit is the encounter: where you meet it, at what hour, and what happens.
A great many yōkai have no body beyond the one drawn for them, and the
catalogue is not diminished by that, because it never promised a diagnosis.

Both systems classify by what a creature is for the person reading. Neither
claims to classify by what it is.

## 珍獸 is a valuation

Now read our own text on 驺吾 (zōuwú):

> 林氏國有珍獸，大若虎，五采畢具，尾長于身，名曰騶吾，乘之日行千里。

The most specific classifier in that line is 珍獸 — a treasure-beast. It is not
a kind. It is a price. The record goes on to give a size by comparison, a
colour count, a proportion, and a use, and the use is the only quantity in it:
a thousand 里 in a day. Filed by what it is worth to whoever owns it.

烛阴 (zhúyīn) is specified the same way and more thoroughly. Nothing in its
record is a measurement of the creature. Everything in it is a measurement of
the observer's day and year — eyes open, day; eyes closed, night; breath out,
winter. It is the most precisely described creature in the book and the
description is entirely about us.

夔 (kuí) would classify perfectly in a *Physiologus*: an ox with one foot whose
light is the sun and moon, whose voice is thunder, and whose movement through
water brings wind and rain. Read as a sign it is complete. Read as an animal it
is a list of weather. And 九尾狐 (jiǔwěihú), which week 8 showed has no name in
the edition at all, is the one creature here that a significance-based
bestiary handles without difficulty, because significance is exactly what later
readers supplied.

## What 其状如 was always doing

其狀如馬. Like a horse. The slot is only informative to somebody who has seen a
horse, which means every comparison in the book is a proposition about the
reader's prior acquaintance. We have been calling it a morphological character
since week 2. It is a statement of familiarity.

Two hours. The first is spent reclassifying four of our creatures into the
other two systems, in pairs, with the entry forms supplied. The second is spent
on the reverse: taking three entries from the other traditions and forcing them
into ours, which fails in ways worth writing down.

## The essay

The reclassification essay is due at the end of this week and is worth 15 per
cent. Take one creature, move it into one of the two systems above, and say
what the move costs and what it recovers. An essay arguing that nothing is lost
has not looked hard enough.
