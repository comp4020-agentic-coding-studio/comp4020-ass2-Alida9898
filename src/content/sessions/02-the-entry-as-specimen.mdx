## Why this week exists

Week 1's first move was **choose characters**. We skipped the hard part: you
cannot choose from nothing.

Linnaeus had it easy. Every flower has stamens, so "how many stamens" is
available for every case before he starts. Material nobody wrote for you rarely
hands you anything that clean.

So this week is the step underneath step one. Where do candidate characters come
from, how do you tell a usable one from a plausible one, and what do you do when
the material simply does not say.

## Candidates come from repetition

You do not invent characters. You notice the material keeps saying the same kind
of thing, and you make a column out of it.

The first pass over any material is therefore counting, not close reading. Which
phrases recur? A shape that repeats is a candidate. A shape that appears twice is
an anecdote.

The 山海经 repeats heavily. Its records are built on:

> 其状如 X，Y 首，见则 Z
>
> shaped like X, with Y for a head, and when it appears, Z

A comparison, a distinguishing part, a consequence. Three candidates, handed
over.

## Three tests a candidate has to survive

**Comparable.** You can check it on every case and get an answer of the same
kind. "Number of legs" is comparable. "Impressive" is not.

**Checkable.** Another reader, same material, same answer. If two readers
disagree and neither is wrong, it is not a character yet.

**About the thing.** It describes what the thing is. Not what it is worth, not
what it does for you, not where you found it. This is the test Borges'
encyclopedist fails fourteen times running.

<Caution label="The most useful-looking candidate is usually relational">
  The characters that feel most informative are often about the relationship
  between the thing and a person: what it is worth, what it is for, what happens
  to you.

  They feel informative because they are what you would act on. That is exactly
  why they are not characters of the thing. Week 5 takes two of them apart.
</Caution>

## Ruling the columns

This is the card you are filling in, for 鹿蜀 (lùshǔ) from the first route:

<Specimen name="鹿蜀" />

Now the working out. The line reads:

> 有獸焉，其狀如馬而白首，其文如虎而赤尾，其音如謠，其名曰鹿蜀，佩之宜子孫。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a horse with a white head, marked like a
> tiger, with a red tail, and its cry is like singing. It is called 鹿蜀. Wear
> it and your descendants will prosper.*

Ruled into the slots the formula offers:

| slot | value |
|---|---|
| comparison | 馬, a horse |
| distinguishing part | 白首, white head. Also tiger markings, red tail |
| name | 鹿蜀 |
| consequence | 佩之宜子孫, worn, it benefits descendants |

Now 蛊雕 (gǔdiāo):

> 水有獸焉，名曰蠱雕，其狀如雕而有角，其音如嬰兒之音，是食人。
>
> *In the water there is a beast called 蠱雕, shaped like an eagle but with
> horns, its cry like an infant's. It eats people.*

Same slots, but the name arrives **first** and the consequence is not an omen. It
is a behaviour: it eats people.

<Caution label="The third slot is not one thing">
  The consequence slot takes at least three unrelated kinds of content. An omen
  (見則天下大旱, a drought follows), a use (佩之宜子孫, wear it for this
  benefit), and a plain behaviour (是食人, it eats people).

  Ruling them into one column makes them look comparable. They are not. Week 5
  is spent taking this column apart, so record now which kind each of your
  records has.
</Caution>

## Count before you trust it

Week 1's lesson was that a classification can be wrong and you can say why. Apply
that to our own candidate before building on it.

This course cites **45 base-text lines**. Of those:

| slot | records carrying it |
|---|---|
| 其狀如, the comparison | 21 of 45 |
| 名曰, the name | 26 of 45 |
| 見則, the omen | 11 of 45 |
| comparison **and** name together | 13 of 45 |

The formula is not a law the text obeys. It is a template the text reaches for
and completes fewer than half the time.

<Caution label="What that number is and is not">
  Those 45 lines were selected for this course's 25 creatures, not sampled from
  the whole book. They show the formula is incomplete in our own data. They do
  **not** give the proportion across all eighteen 卷, and no page here claims
  they do.
</Caution>

## Missing is not zero

夫诸 (fūzhū) has a comparison, a name and an omen, and **no use**:

> 有獸焉，其狀如白鹿而四角，名曰夫諸，見則其邑大水。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a white deer with four horns. It is
> called 夫諸. Where it appears, that town floods.*

軨軨 (línglíng) has all three and nothing else:

> 有獸焉，其狀如牛而虎文，其音如欽。其名曰軨軨，其鳴自訆，見則天下大水。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like an ox with tiger markings, its cry like
> a groan. It is called 軨軨, it calls its own name, and where it appears, there
> are floods.*

Two floods, two scopes: 其邑 is one town, 天下 is everywhere. Same slot,
different magnitude, and the formula has nowhere to record the difference.

夫诸's missing use is not "no benefit". It is **no information**. Three different
things can put a blank in a cell, and a system that stores them identically has
destroyed information:

- the material says the value is nothing
- the material does not say
- nobody has checked yet

Only the first is a value. The other two are states of your own knowledge.

<Caution label="Writing none is a claim">
  If the record is silent and your table says `none`, you have added a fact the
  material does not support. Six weeks later neither you nor a reader will know
  which it was.

  This is the one thing the first assessment fails outright, and it is not a
  technicality. A taxonomy that cannot tell its inferences from its sources is
  not evidence of anything.
</Caution>

## What you leave with

Column names, agreed in the room and kept all semester. Name them now, while
they are still arguable: an unnamed column gets quietly widened every time
something does not fit, and nobody notices when it starts holding four unrelated
kinds of thing.

Your first specimen card is due Sunday, for any creature in 南山經 other than
鹿蜀. It cites its line, fills the slots that line supplies, and leaves the rest
blank. Marked on one thing: whether the blanks are honest.
