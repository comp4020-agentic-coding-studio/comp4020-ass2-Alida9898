## What this week does

Week 7 showed a name outliving its record. This week the name field fails while
the record is still in front of you, and it fails in two directions at once. A
name can take more creatures than one. A creature can take more names than one.
The entry form has a single <code>name</code> field and cannot hold either fact.

Four sections, two for each direction:

1. **One name, three creatures** — <span lang="zh-Hans">肥遗</span>, over two
   incompatible snakes and a bird.
2. **One creature, two bodies** — <span lang="zh-Hans">窫窳</span>, with
   <span lang="zh-Hans">旋龟</span> and <span lang="zh-Hans">天吴</span> doing
   the same at two smaller scales.
3. **Names that multiply** — <span lang="zh-Hans">并封</span>,
   <span lang="zh-Hans">相柳</span> and <span lang="zh-Hans">讙</span>.
4. **Why both obvious repairs assume the thing in dispute.**

## Example 1: one name, three creatures

<Specimen name="肥遗" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">肥遗</span> (féiyí) has three records.
<span lang="zh-Hant">北山經之首</span> gives a snake with one head and two
bodies, at <span lang="zh-Hant">渾夕之山</span>, and a national drought.
<span lang="zh-Hant">西山經之首</span> gives a bird at
<span lang="zh-Hant">英山</span>, quail-shaped, yellow-bodied with a red bill,
which cures pestilence and kills vermin. Also in
<span lang="zh-Hant">西山經之首</span>, at
<span lang="zh-Hant">太華之山</span>, a snake again — a different one, six feet
and four wings, spelled <span lang="zh-Hant">肥𧔥</span>, with a total drought.

Lay the three side by side. One snake has two bodies and no legs; the other has
six legs and four wings. One omen is national and the other is total. The bird
has no omen and is the only one of the three with a use. There is no character
on which any two of them agree.

<span lang="zh-Hans">郭璞</span> (Guō Pú) saw this in the fourth century and
wrote <span lang="zh-Hant">疑是同名</span> — a suspicion that the name is merely
shared. Not a ruling. The oldest commentator on the text reached for the escape
hatch this session is about to reach for, and declined to close it.

## Example 2: one creature, two bodies

<Specimen name="窫窳" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">窫窳</span> (yàyǔ) fails more quietly.
<span lang="zh-Hant">海內南經</span> puts it in the
<span lang="zh-Hant">弱水</span> with a dragon's head, shaped like a
<span lang="zh-Hant">貙</span>, eating people.
<span lang="zh-Hant">海內西經</span> makes it snake-bodied and human-faced and
already dead, killed by the servant of
<span lang="zh-Hant">貳負</span>. Neither record is defective. They cannot be
the same body.

The same failure runs at two smaller scales.

<Specimen name="旋龟" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">旋龟</span> (xuánguī) is viper-tailed at
<span lang="zh-Hant">杻陽之山</span> and turtle-tailed at
<span lang="zh-Hant">密山</span>, with the sound of splitting wood in both.

<Specimen name="天吴" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">天吴</span> (tiānwú) disagrees with itself across the
boundary week 6 spent an hour on: a <span lang="zh-Hant">獸</span> with eight
tails in <span lang="zh-Hant">海外東經</span>, a
<span lang="zh-Hant">神人</span>, a god-person, with ten in
<span lang="zh-Hant">大荒東經</span>. Eight heads is the only character that
survives both readings.

So the disagreements are graded. One tail-tip, one tail count and one kind-word
are survivable; a dragon's head against a human face is not. Nothing in the
entry form marks where on that scale a given conflict sits.

## Example 3: names that multiply

The second direction is easier to see and harder to repair.

<Specimen name="并封" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">并封</span> (bìngfēng) is a black pig with a head at each
end in <span lang="zh-Hant">海外西經</span>. In
<span lang="zh-Hant">大荒西經</span> a two-headed beast is named
<span lang="zh-Hant">屏蓬</span>, with no pig and no colour — and the heads are
on the other axis, <span lang="zh-Hant">左右</span>, left and right, rather than
<span lang="zh-Hant">前後</span>, front and back. The two records usually read as one creature
under two names do not agree about the one feature they are supposed to share.

<Specimen name="相柳" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">相柳</span> (xiāngliǔ) is nine human-faced heads on a
green snake's body in <span lang="zh-Hant">海外北經</span>, and
<span lang="zh-Hant">相繇</span>, minister to
<span lang="zh-Hant">共工</span>, in <span lang="zh-Hant">大荒北經</span>.

<Specimen name="讙" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">讙</span> (huān) shows why this is not a spelling problem.
The character names a one-eyed, three-tailed wildcat at
<span lang="zh-Hant">翼望之山</span>, and it also names mountains and a country
elsewhere in the text. Four referents, and the text notices no collision at all,
because the text is not indexing by name.

## Why both obvious repairs assume the thing in dispute

Adding a <code>synonyms</code> field looks like the fix. It is not. A synonym
list asserts that two names denote one thing, which is the conclusion under
dispute, and it writes that conclusion into a data field where it stops looking
like a claim.

Splitting <span lang="zh-Hans">肥遗</span> into three species asserts the
opposite — that the names are unrelated — which is precisely what
<span lang="zh-Hans">郭璞</span> declined to assert with better access to the
text than anybody in this room.

<Caution label="The failure is in the form, not in the text">
  The text is not confused. It is not indexing by name, so a name colliding with
  another name costs it nothing.

  The failure belongs to the entry form, which assumed a name picks out a kind
  and put one field there to prove it. Every system in week 12 inherits this
  assumption unless it says otherwise in writing.
</Caution>

## What you leave with

The field you could not design, written down as a paragraph naming what it would
have to do: hold a suspicion that two names denote one creature, without
recording that suspicion as a fact, and survive being sorted.

Nobody has produced one in this session yet, and the paragraph is the artefact
rather than a failure to produce one. Week 9 looks at two bestiaries that never
had this problem, because neither of them ever claimed that a name picks out a
kind.
