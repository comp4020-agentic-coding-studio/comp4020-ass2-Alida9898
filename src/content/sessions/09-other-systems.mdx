## What this week does

Week 8 left the name field broken and no repair that did not assume its own
conclusion. Before rebuilding, this week reads two bestiaries that were never
built the way ours was, and asks the uncomfortable question in the other
direction: not whether they are worse, but whether ours has been one of them all
along.

Three sections:

1. **Two systems that never needed a species concept**, and what each puts at
   the top of its division.
2. **A classifier that is a price** — <span lang="zh-Hans">驺吾</span>, where
   our own material makes the same move.
3. **What <span lang="zh-Hant">其狀如</span> was always doing** —
   <span lang="zh-Hans">烛阴</span>, and a column this course has leaned on
   since week 2.

## Two systems that never needed a species concept

The medieval European bestiary descends from the *Physiologus*. Its entries
describe an animal and then say what the animal means. The lion, the pelican and
the phoenix sit in one book on equal terms, because the criterion for inclusion
is signification and not existence. Nobody in that tradition is embarrassed by
the phoenix. Signification is the field, and the phoenix fills it well.

Japanese <span lang="ja">妖怪</span> catalogues sort differently again.
<span lang="ja">鳥山石燕</span>'s <span lang="ja">画図百鬼夜行</span> and its
successors give each creature a name, a picture, and often nothing else. The
unit is the encounter: where you meet it, at what hour, and what happens to you.
A great many of them have no body beyond the one drawn for them, and the
catalogue is not diminished by that, because it never promised a diagnosis.

Both sort by what a creature is for the person reading. Neither claims to sort
by what it is.

<Caution label="Diagnostic, not inferior">
  Read these two for their organising principle, not as earlier attempts at
  what we are doing. Neither was trying to produce a diagnosis and failing.

  The reason to read them at all is that a system's top division is the hardest
  part of it to see from inside. Ours is easier to see once you have looked at
  two that chose differently.
</Caution>

## 珍獸 is a price, not a kind

<Specimen name="驺吾" />

The most specific classifier in that line is
<span lang="zh-Hant">珍獸</span>, a treasure-beast. It is not a kind. It is a
price. The record goes on to give a size by comparison, a colour count and a
proportion, and then the only quantity in it: a thousand
<span lang="zh-Hant">里</span> in a day, which is a measurement of the journey
somebody else takes. Filed by what it is worth to whoever owns it.

<Specimen name="夔" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">夔</span> (kuí) would classify perfectly in a
*Physiologus*. An ox with one foot whose light is the sun and moon, whose voice
is thunder, and whose passage through water brings wind and rain: read as a sign
the entry is complete. Read as an animal it is a list of weather.

<span lang="zh-Hans">九尾狐</span> (jiǔwěihú), which week 7 showed has no name
in the edition at all, is the creature a signification-based bestiary handles
most easily of any in our bestiary. Significance is exactly what later readers
supplied to it. The system that files it best is the one whose field our records
never filled.

## What 其状如 was always doing

<span lang="zh-Hant">其狀如馬</span>. Like a horse. That slot is informative
only to somebody who has seen a horse, which makes every comparison in the book
a proposition about the reader's prior acquaintance. This course has called it a
morphological character since week 2. It is a statement of familiarity, and week
3 already met the case that proves it: the comparison slot on
<span lang="zh-Hans">帝江</span> holds a sack.

<Specimen name="烛阴" />

<span lang="zh-Hans">烛阴</span> (zhúyīn) makes the same point at the other end
of the entry. Exactly one clause in that record measures the creature:
<span lang="zh-Hant">身長千里</span>, a thousand
<span lang="zh-Hant">里</span> long. Every other clause specifies what its
states do to an observer — eyes open are day, eyes closed are night, breath out
is winter and in is summer, and its resting would be wind. It is among the most
precisely specified records in the book, and the precision is almost entirely
about us.

So the answer to the question this week opened with is yes. Our top division is
not a fact about the reader in the way the *Physiologus*'s is, but two of our
columns are, and we did not put them there deliberately. We inherited them from
a text that was answering a different question.

## What you leave with

One creature moved into one of the two systems above, with what the move costs
and what it recovers, which is the
[reclassification essay](/assessments/reclassification-essay/) due at the end of
week 10. An essay arguing that nothing is lost has not looked hard enough.

Also a list, for your own system, of every column that encodes a reader rather
than a creature. There are at least two. Week 10 runs creatures through two
systems at once and compares what each filing threw away, which is the test that
turns this week's reading into a result.
