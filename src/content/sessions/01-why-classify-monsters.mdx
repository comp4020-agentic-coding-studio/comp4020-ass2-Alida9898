## Why this week exists

You already classify things. A folder structure, a way of sorting laundry, a
mental shelf for people you have met once. Most of these you built without
deciding anything, and most fail the same way: two things that belong together
end up apart and you cannot say why.

This course is about doing it on purpose. By week 12 you build a system for
material nobody has handed you one for.

Today: what the material is, and what the method is. No classifying yet.

## What the <span lang="zh-Hans">山海经</span> is

The <span lang="zh-Hans">山海经</span> (Shānhǎijīng, the *Classic of Mountains
and Seas*) is a Chinese geographical survey, compiled in pieces between roughly
the fourth century BCE and the first century CE. Nobody knows who wrote it. It
survives in eighteen scrolls, and it reads like an itinerary: go three hundred
<span lang="zh-Hans">里</span> east, there is a mountain called X, on it grows
this, in its river lives that.

Among the plants and the ore and the rivers, it describes hundreds of animals.
Some are ordinary. Many are not. It is the earliest written source for a good
deal of Chinese mythology, but it is not written as mythology. It is written as
a survey, in the flat voice of somebody recording what is where.

That flatness is why it is our material. Here is one entry, printed the way this
course prints every one:

That is the whole record. A comparison to an animal you are assumed to know, a
distinguishing part, a name, a place, and sometimes a consequence. No author to
ask, no second observer, nothing to measure.

## How Linnaeus classified

Now the method. Before the eighteenth century a plant's name was a diagnostic
phrase, a string of Latin adjectives long enough to tell it from its neighbours.
A description pretending to be a label. It grew whenever somebody found a
similar plant, it differed between authors, and you could not alphabetise it.

Linnaeus made three moves. Any classification has to make all three.

**He chose characters.** A small set of features you can check on every specimen.
How many stamens, mammary glands or not, how many toes. The choice is the whole
game, and it is a choice: he sorted plants by their sexual parts, which was
neither obvious nor uncontroversial at the time.

**He nested them into ranks.** Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus,
species. Each one inside the last.

**He gave two-part names.** Genus plus species. *Homo sapiens*. One name, one
kind, everywhere.

What does the nesting buy? Economy.

<Hierarchy />

Say *Homo sapiens* and you have also said primate, mammal, chordate, animal,
without writing any of it down. Six statements ride along with the name. A flat
list carries none of that, which is why a folder with two hundred subfolders is
unusable and one with six is not.

Underneath all three moves is an assumption about evidence. Linnaeus had
specimens. Where he had none he had a drawing, and where he had neither he had
the written testimony of somebody who claimed to have stood next to the animal.
The system takes for granted that behind every name there is a body somebody
could in principle produce.

The <span lang="zh-Hans">山海经</span> produces no bodies. Every difficulty in
the remaining eleven weeks comes from that.

## Borges' fake Chinese encyclopedia

One counter-example, so you know what a bad system looks like.

In a 1942 essay on John Wilkins, Borges describes a Chinese encyclopedia that
does not exist, the Celestial Emporium of Benevolent Knowledge, and quotes its
division of animals into fourteen classes. Among them: those belonging to the
Emperor, embalmed ones, suckling pigs, mermaids, stray dogs, those drawn with a
very fine camel-hair brush, those that resemble flies from a distance, and those
included in this classification.

Foucault put it on the first page of *Les mots et les choses* and said he
laughed out loud.

Why is it funny? Not because the animals are strange. Mermaids are no odder than
a platypus. It is funny because every category sorts by a different question. Who
owns it. What state it is in. What it looks like from far away. Whether it is on
this list. Linnaeus counted stamens every single time.

So you have a test, and you will use it all semester. Take any category and ask
what question it answers. If two categories in one system answer different kinds
of question, the system has a level problem.

<Caution label="Arbitrary is not the same as broken">
  The Borges list is usually produced to prove that all classification is
  arbitrary, so no system is better than any other.

  Watch what that does to itself. The joke works only because you can tell the
  Emporium is bad, which means you applied a standard, which means one exists.
  Arbitrary means the boundary could have been drawn elsewhere, and nearly every
  boundary could. Broken means the system cannot do the job it claims. Only the
  second is fatal, and weeks 9 to 12 turn on the difference.
</Caution>

## What the book already sorts by

Last thing. The <span lang="zh-Hans">山海经</span> is usually called a chaotic
catalogue of monsters. It is not chaotic, and you cannot improve on a system
until you can say what the existing one does.

Start with the cover. <span lang="zh-Hant">山</span> is mountain,
<span lang="zh-Hant">海</span> is sea, <span lang="zh-Hant">經</span> is classic,
and the book divides exactly where its name divides: five scrolls of mountains,
thirteen of seas. The top level of the classification is printed on the title
page.

Almost every chapter title after that is a compass direction:
<span lang="zh-Hant">南</span> south, <span lang="zh-Hant">西</span> west,
<span lang="zh-Hant">北</span> north, <span lang="zh-Hant">東</span> east,
<span lang="zh-Hant">中</span> centre. Five <span lang="zh-Hant">山經</span>
(shānjīng) hold twenty-six numbered routes between them. Four
<span lang="zh-Hant">海外經</span> (hǎiwàijīng) cover what lies beyond the seas
and four <span lang="zh-Hant">海內經</span> (hǎinèijīng) what lies within, each
set running south, west, north, east. Four
<span lang="zh-Hant">大荒經</span> (dàhuāngjīng) cover the great wilderness,
running east, south, west, north. The eighteenth is headed
<span lang="zh-Hant">海內經</span> again with no direction at all.

Inside a route, each creature is filed under the mountain or water where it is
found. Never under anything about its body.

So what question does this system answer? What will I meet if I go there. And it
answers that completely. By the test above, that is a classification: one
character, applied to every entry, never changing question halfway. Which is more
than the Celestial Emporium managed.

Here is what it costs. <span lang="zh-Hans">狌狌</span>,
<span lang="zh-Hans">鹿蜀</span> (lùshǔ), <span lang="zh-Hans">猼訑</span>
(bóshī) and <span lang="zh-Hans">九尾狐</span> (jiǔwěihú) all stand on the first
route, <span lang="zh-Hant">南山經之首</span> (nánshānjīng zhī shǒu), on four
mountains in the order you would walk past them. Two of them have nine tails.
<span lang="zh-Hans">猼訑</span> has them on a sheep:

> <span lang="zh-Hant">有獸焉，其狀如羊，九尾四耳，其目在背，其名曰猼訑，佩之不畏。</span>
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a sheep, with nine tails and four ears,
> and its eyes on its back. It is called <span lang="zh-Hant">猼訑</span>. Wear
> it and you will not be afraid.*

The beast four mountains further on has them on a fox:

> <span lang="zh-Hant">有獸焉，其狀如狐而九尾，其音如嬰兒，能食人；食者不蠱。</span>
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a fox but with nine tails, and its cry is
> like an infant's. It can eat people. Eat it and you will not be harmed by
> <span lang="zh-Hant">蠱</span> (gǔ), poisoning by witchcraft.*

The text never mentions that they share a count. A system sorting by body would
have put them side by side. This one files <span lang="zh-Hans">猼訑</span> next
to whatever else stands on <span lang="zh-Hant">基山</span>. That disagreement
is the course.

## What you leave with

Names for the slots in a record, argued in the room. Week 2 formalises them and
the rest of the semester keeps them.

And one rule that never bends, starting now: cite the line, or declare which
line you could not find. A plausible-sounding quotation with nothing behind it
is the single thing that fails an assessment outright. We are building a system
for a text nobody can re-examine, so the citation is all the evidence there is.
