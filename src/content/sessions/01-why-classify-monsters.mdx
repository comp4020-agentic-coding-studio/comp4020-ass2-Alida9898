Classification is a choice, and different choices show different things. The
山海经 (Shānhǎijīng, the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*) chose geography.
Linnaeus chose form. This course spends twelve weeks finding out whether the
first can be translated into the second, and what is lost in the transfer.

This session sets up the comparison. It takes one system that works, one that
does not, and then the ordering the text has already imposed on itself.

## What Linnaeus actually did

Before the eighteenth century an animal's name was a sentence of description,
and it differed from country to country. Linnaeus made three moves, and they are
the three moves any classification has to make.

He **chose characters**: a small set of features that can be compared across
cases, such as the number of stamens in a flower or the presence of mammary
glands. He **built a hierarchy**: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus,
species, each nested inside the last. He **gave two-part names**: genus plus
species, one name per kind, the same name everywhere.

The nesting is the part worth looking at, because it is the part the 山海经 does
not have.

Read from the outside in, each box narrows the claim and each character is the
same kind of fact as the one before it. Read from the inside out, a name carries
six further statements that were never written next to it. That economy is what
a hierarchy buys.

Underneath all three is an assumption about evidence. Linnaeus had specimens.
Where he had no specimen he had a drawing, and where he had neither he had the
written testimony of someone who claimed to have stood next to the animal. The
system takes for granted that behind every name there is a body somebody could
in principle produce.

The 山海经 produces no bodies. It offers a formula and a location: a creature is
introduced as being like something the reader is presumed to know, distinguished
from that by a part or two, named, and assigned a consequence. There is no author
to ask, no specimen to measure, and no second observer. Every difficulty in the
remaining eleven weeks comes from that gap.

## The encyclopedia Borges invented

In a 1942 essay on John Wilkins, Borges describes a Chinese encyclopedia that
does not exist, and quotes its division of animals. The categories include those
belonging to the Emperor, embalmed ones, trained ones, suckling pigs, mermaids,
stray dogs, those drawn with a very fine camel-hair brush, those that resemble
flies from a distance, and — the one that does the most damage — those included
in this classification. Foucault put the passage on the first page of *Les mots
et les choses* in 1966 and reported laughing at it.

The list is not absurd because its animals are strange. It is absurd because its
characters are, one after another, drawn from different levels. Ownership is not
the same kind of fact as physical condition. Physical condition is not the same
kind of fact as appearance at a distance. And a category defined by membership in
the list that contains it is not a fact about animals at all. Linnaeus counted
stamens in every case; Borges' encyclopedist changes the question with every
entry.

The list is usually produced as evidence that all classification is arbitrary.
The opposite lesson is available and is the one this course takes. The joke works
only because a reader can tell that these categories fail, and can say why. A
classification that can be wrong is a thing that can be done well.

Hold the list in view for twelve weeks and a second result arrives. The 山海经 is
much closer to Linnaeus than to Borges. Its characters are real and they are
consistent: a body compared to a known animal, a distinguishing part, a place, a
consequence. What it lacks is not information but hierarchy — nothing in the text
groups its creatures above the level of the place they stand.

## What the text sorts by

The 山海经 is commonly treated as raw material waiting for a system. This week's
rule denies that, and the denial is checkable against the table of contents.

Start with the title. 山 is mountain, 海 is sea, 經 is classic, and the book
divides exactly where its name does: five chapters of mountains, thirteen of
seas. The top level of the classification is printed on the cover.

The received text has eighteen 卷 (juàn, scrolls), and the arrangement is
geographic from the first to the last. Almost every chapter title is a
direction: 南 south, 西 west, 北 north, 東 east, 中 centre.

Five are the 山經 (shānjīng, mountain classics): 南山經 south, 西山經 west,
北山經 north, 東山經 east, 中山經 centre. Between them they hold twenty-six
numbered routes, each route a sequence of mountains given in compass bearings
at stated distances. Four more are the 海外經 (hǎiwàijīng), the classics of
what lies beyond the seas, and four the 海內經 (hǎinèijīng), of what lies
within them; each set of four runs south, west, north, east. Four are the
大荒經 (dàhuāngjīng), the classics of the great wilderness, which run east,
south, west, north — the same principle, a different rotation. The eighteenth
is headed 海內經 again, with no direction attached to it at all.

Within a route, the unit of filing is the mountain or the water, not the kind of
animal. A creature belongs to the place it is met at. Nothing in the text groups
a creature with any other creature on the strength of its body. The arrangement
answers one question and answers it completely: what will I meet if I go there.

That is a classification. It has characters, it applies them without exception,
and it is about the world rather than about the page. It is not the absence of a
system, and reading it as an absence is the first mistake this material makes
available. The course's work is to swap its geographic ordering for a Linnaean
ordering by form, and then to look at what that reveals and what it destroys.

## Four records on one route

狌狌 (xīngxīng), 鹿蜀 (lùshǔ), 猼訑 (bóshī) and 九尾狐 (jiǔwěihú) are all filed
in 南山經之首 (nánshānjīng zhī shǒu), the head of the southern mountain
classic, which is the first route in the book. They stand on four mountains
along it, in this order: 招搖之山 (zhāoyáo), 杻陽之山 (niǔyáng), 基山 (jī) and
青丘之山 (qīngqiū). The suffix 之山 means "mountain of". The creatures appear
in that order because that is the order the route passes the mountains.
Nothing else determines it.

Two of them have nine tails. 猼訑 has them on a sheep, at 基山:

> 有獸焉，其狀如羊，九尾四耳，其目在背，其名曰猼訑，佩之不畏。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a sheep, with nine tails and four ears,
> and its eyes on its back. It is called 猼訑. Wear it and you will not be
> afraid.*

The beast at 青丘之山 has them on a fox:

> 有獸焉，其狀如狐而九尾，其音如嬰兒，能食人；食者不蠱。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a fox but with nine tails, and its cry
> is like an infant's. It can eat people. Eat it and you will not be harmed by
> 蠱 (gǔ), poisoning by witchcraft.*

The italic line under each quotation is this course's own translation, printed
inside the quotation so that it cannot be mistaken for the record. It is not a
substitute for the line above it. The quoted lines stay in the traditional
characters the edition prints, because changing the script of a quotation is
still changing the quotation.

The edition never calls that second one 九尾狐. It is an unnamed beast, and the
name it is filed under here arrived later; week 8 is about how.

The two records sit several mountains apart, and the text nowhere remarks that
they share a count. A system sorting by body would have set them side by side. A
system sorting by route sets 猼訑 beside whatever else stands on 基山. The
disagreement between those two filings is what the whole course runs on.

## In the session

Two hours, following the three parts above.

- **Forty minutes** on Linnaeus and on Borges, taken together, with the three
  moves named and the encyclopedia read against them. The question put to the
  room is which level each Borges category sorts by, entry by entry.
- **Forty minutes** on the 招搖之山 passage, read slowly. We mark up the formula
  and name its slots. Those names are provisional and we keep them all semester,
  so they are worth arguing about now rather than in week 9.
- **Forty minutes** in groups, sorting ten creatures from 南山經 with no rules
  supplied. The point is to notice which rules you reach for unprompted, and
  whether any of them is one the text itself uses.

## The card that does not count

You leave with the slot names the room agreed on, which week 2 formalises, and
with a specimen card for 狌狌 that you do not hand in. The first card that
counts is due at the end of week 2.
