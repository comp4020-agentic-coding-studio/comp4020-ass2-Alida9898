## Why this week exists

You already classify things. A folder structure, a way of sorting laundry, a
mental shelf for people you have met once. Most of these you built without
deciding anything, and most fail the same way: two things that belong together
end up apart and you cannot say why.

This course is about doing it on purpose, and by week 12 you build a system for
material nobody has handed you one for. Week 1 is the toolkit, and the first look
at what we are going to use it on.

## What Linnaeus actually did

Before the eighteenth century a plant's name was a diagnostic phrase, a string of
Latin adjectives long enough to tell it from its neighbours. It is a description
pretending to be a label. It grows whenever somebody finds a similar plant, it
differs between authors, and you cannot alphabetise it.

Linnaeus made three moves, and they are the three moves any classification has to
make.

**He chose characters.** A small set of features you can check on every specimen:
how many stamens, mammary glands or not, how many toes. The choice is the whole
game, and it is a choice. He sorted plants by their sexual parts, which was
neither obvious nor uncontroversial.

**He nested them into ranks.** Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus,
species, each inside the last.

**He gave two-part names.** Genus plus species. *Homo sapiens*. One name, one
kind, everywhere.

What does the nesting actually buy? Economy.

<Hierarchy />

Say *Homo sapiens* and you have also said primate, mammal, chordate, animal
without writing any of it down. Six statements ride along with the name. A flat
list carries none of that, which is why a folder structure with two hundred
folders at the top level is unusable and one with six is not.

Underneath all three moves sits an assumption about evidence. Linnaeus had
specimens; where he had none he had a drawing, and where he had neither he had
the written testimony of someone who claimed to have stood next to the animal.
The system takes for granted that behind every name there is a body somebody
could in principle produce.

## Our specimen is a record

The 山海经 (Shānhǎijīng, the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*) produces no bodies.
What it produces is entries, so the entry is our specimen. This is one, printed
the way every week of this course prints its own:

Read the fields and you have read the raw material of the whole semester. A
comparison to an animal you are assumed to know, a distinguishing part, a name, a
place, sometimes a consequence. No author to ask, no second observer, nothing to
measure. Every difficulty in the remaining eleven weeks comes from that.

  A taxonomy that hides which parts it made up is the thing this course is
  against.

## The encyclopedia Borges invented

In a 1942 essay on John Wilkins, Borges describes a Chinese encyclopedia that
does not exist, the Celestial Emporium of Benevolent Knowledge, and quotes its
division of animals into fourteen classes. Among them: those belonging to the
Emperor, embalmed ones, suckling pigs, mermaids, stray dogs, those drawn with a
very fine camel-hair brush, those that resemble flies from a distance, and those
included in this classification.

Foucault put it on the first page of *Les mots et les choses* and said he laughed
out loud.

Why is it funny? Not because the animals are strange; mermaids are no odder than
a platypus. It is funny because every category sorts by a different kind of
question. Who owns it. What state it is in. What it looks like from far away.
Whether it is on this list. Linnaeus counted stamens every single time.

That gives you a test you will use all semester. Take any category and ask what
question it answers. If two categories in one system answer different kinds of
question, the system has a level problem.

<Caution label="Arbitrary is not the same as broken">
  The Borges list is usually produced to prove all classification is arbitrary,
  so no system is better than any other.

  Watch what that argument does to itself. The joke works only because you can
  tell the Emporium is bad, which means you applied a standard, which means one
  exists. Arbitrary means the boundary could have been drawn elsewhere, and
  nearly every boundary could. Broken means the system cannot do the job it
  claims. Only the second is fatal, and weeks 9 to 12 depend on the difference.
</Caution>

## What the text already sorts by

The 山海经 is usually called a chaotic catalogue of monsters. It is not chaotic,
and you cannot improve on a system until you can say what the existing one does.

Start with the cover. 山 is mountain, 海 is sea, 經 is classic, and the book
divides exactly where its name divides: five chapters of mountains, thirteen of
seas. The top level of the classification is printed on the title page.

The received text has eighteen 卷 (juàn, scrolls), and almost every chapter title
is a compass direction: 南 south, 西 west, 北 north, 東 east, 中 centre.

- Five 山經 (shānjīng, mountain classics), holding twenty-six numbered routes
- Four 海外經 (hǎiwàijīng), beyond the seas, running south, west, north, east
- Four 海內經 (hǎinèijīng), within them, same rotation
- Four 大荒經 (dàhuāngjīng), the great wilderness, running east, south, west,
  north
- One more, headed 海內經 again, with no direction at all

Inside a route each creature is filed under the mountain or water where it is
found, never under anything about its body. So what question does this system
answer? What will I meet if I go there. And it answers that completely.

By the test above, that is a classification. It has a character, it applies it to
every entry, and it never changes question halfway, which is more than the
Celestial Emporium managed.

## What that filing costs

狌狌, 鹿蜀 (lùshǔ), 猼訑 (bóshī) and 九尾狐 (jiǔwěihú) all stand on the same
route, 南山經之首 (nánshānjīng zhī shǒu), on four mountains in this order:
招搖之山 (zhāoyáo), 杻陽之山 (niǔyáng), 基山 (jī) and 青丘之山 (qīngqiū). The
suffix 之山 means "mountain of". They appear in that order because that is the
order you would walk past them.

Two of them have nine tails. 猼訑 has them on a sheep, at 基山:

> 有獸焉，其狀如羊，九尾四耳，其目在背，其名曰猼訑，佩之不畏。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a sheep, with nine tails and four ears,
> and its eyes on its back. It is called 猼訑. Wear it and you will not be
> afraid.*

The beast at 青丘之山 has them on a fox:

> 有獸焉，其狀如狐而九尾，其音如嬰兒，能食人；食者不蠱。
>
> *There is a beast there, shaped like a fox but with nine tails, and its cry is
> like an infant's. It can eat people. Eat it and you will not be harmed by 蠱
> (gǔ), poisoning by witchcraft.*

Two records, several mountains apart, sharing a feature the text never mentions
they share. A system sorting by body would have filed them together. This one
files 猼訑 next to whatever else stands on 基山. That disagreement is the course.

The edition never calls the second one 九尾狐. It is an unnamed beast, and the
name it is filed under here arrived centuries later. Week 7 is about how.

## What you leave with

Column names for the record's slots, argued in the room, which week 2 formalises
and the whole semester keeps.

One rule never bends, starting now: you cite the line, or you declare which line
you could not find. A plausible-sounding quotation with nothing behind it is the
single thing that fails an assessment outright. We are building a system for a
text nobody can re-examine, so the citation is all the evidence there is.
